Power & Utility

Serving utility: Grant, Douglas & Chelan County PUDs · Grid: WECC / BPA region

PUD rates remain enviable but headroom is constrained; new large-load requests face allocation policies, higher 'evolving industry' rate classes, and queue discipline. BPA regional capacity and new transmission are the long-term unlock.

Entitlement Climate

Quincy and Grant County remain practically accommodating (data centers are the tax base); permitting is navigable. SEPA review applies — schedule it, don't fear it.

Water & Cooling

Columbia Basin supply with municipal/industrial allocations; Quincy has history with reclaimed/cooling solutions. Far better than Phoenix; still paper it carefully.

Tax & Incentives

Washington rural-county data center sales/use tax exemption (eligibility windows and certification requirements have shifted repeatedly — verify current program status and sunset). No state income tax; B&O and REET planning matter on the deal itself.

Site Diligence Checklist

Applies to any specific parcel in this market; ordered by typical go/no-go significance.

Power & Utility (35%)

Power is the deal. A site without a credible megawatt story is a farm field with a nice survey. This workstream usually decides go/no-go before anything else is worth spending money on.

☐ Identify the serving utility and confirm exclusive service territory
Why: Territory disputes and unclear service rights between an IOU, a co-op, or a municipal utility can add years. In some states territory is contestable; in others it is fixed by statute or commission order.
Red flags: Site straddles two service territories; co-op territory with limited large-load experience; municipal utility with no transmission-level interconnection history.
☐ Obtain a will-serve or load study commitment in writing, with capacity and timeline
Why: Verbal 'we can serve you' means nothing. The letter should state MW available, voltage, delivery point, required system upgrades, and an estimated energization schedule.
Red flags: Utility will only commit to 'study queue' status; energization date beyond 4-5 years; capacity conditioned on unbuilt generation or transmission.
☐ Review the utility's large-load tariff and any pending large-load rate cases
Why: Multiple states have adopted or proposed special data center tariffs: minimum take-or-pay terms, long contract tenors (10-15 yrs), collateral requirements, and exit fees. These reshape project economics and financeability.
Red flags: Pending rate case that could impose retroactive terms; take-or-pay minimums above realistic ramp; termination fees that survive assignment.
☐ Map distance to existing transmission and substation capacity
Why: A new 230kV+ substation and transmission tap is a multi-year, $50M+ undertaking with its own siting, NEPA/state-EIS, and condemnation risks. Proximity to existing headroom is worth a premium on land price.
Red flags: Nearest transmission line >5 miles; substation expansion requires new right-of-way through residential or environmentally sensitive land.
☐ Assess interconnection queue position and regional (RTO/ISO) constraints
Why: In constrained RTO regions, queue position is an asset that conveys (or doesn't) with the land. Confirm whether any queue rights exist, who holds them, and whether they are assignable.
Red flags: Seller markets 'power available' based on a queue position held by an affiliate that doesn't convey; cluster-study restart risk.
☐ Evaluate behind-the-meter / on-site generation options and their regulatory posture
Why: Gas turbines, fuel cells, and bridging generation can rescue a timeline, but several states are moving to regulate or surcharge behind-the-meter large loads. Air permits for on-site generation are their own critical path.
Red flags: Nonattainment area for NOx/ozone making air permits slow; state legislation pending on BTM large-load cost allocation.
☐ Confirm who pays for system upgrades and on what schedule (CIAC exposure)
Why: Contribution-in-aid-of-construction obligations can run nine figures and are often due before financing milestones. Tax treatment of CIAC matters too.
Red flags: Open-ended upgrade cost true-up clauses; utility requires full prepayment with no refund mechanism if capacity is later shared.
☐ Review curtailment, demand-response, and non-firm service terms
Why: Some fast-track interconnection products are conditioned on curtailability. Acceptable for some tenants, fatal for others — know the end user before accepting non-firm terms.
Red flags: Unlimited curtailment hours; curtailment triggers defined solely at utility discretion.

Land Use, Zoning & Entitlements (20%)

The entitlement climate for data centers shifted hard after 2023. By-right today is not by-right at closing unless you protect it. This is where a CRE lawyer earns the fee.

☐ Confirm current zoning permits data center use by right, by special exception, or not at all
Why: Many jurisdictions have re-classified data centers out of by-right industrial categories or added special-exception requirements with discretionary review.
Red flags: Use is permitted only via legislative rezoning; pending zoning ordinance amendment targeting data centers; 'data center' undefined in the code (interpretation risk).
☐ Check for pending or threatened moratoria at county, city, and town level
Why: Data center moratoria have been adopted with little warning in multiple states. A moratorium between PSA signing and entitlement vesting can strand a deposit.
Red flags: Council member statements or citizen petitions advocating a pause; comprehensive plan update in progress that revisits data center policy.
☐ Determine when development rights vest under state law and structure the PSA around it
Why: Vesting rules vary enormously: some states vest at complete application, others only at building permit. The PSA's diligence and closing conditions should track the actual vesting trigger.
Red flags: Long gap between closing obligation and vesting event; jurisdiction with history of downzoning during application pendency.
☐ Review setback, height, noise, and screening standards specific to data centers
Why: Newer ordinances impose data-center-specific noise limits (often dBA at property line), generator setbacks, and substation screening that can cut buildable envelope materially.
Red flags: Noise standard measured at residential property line with homes <500 ft; generator yard orientation constrained by adjacency.
☐ Identify all discretionary approvals and their realistic timelines (rezoning, SUP, site plan, comp plan amendment)
Why: Each discretionary step is a public hearing, which is an organizing opportunity for opposition. Map the full approval stack and the political calendar (elections matter).
Red flags: Approval requires supermajority after protest petition; hearing schedule pushes decision past an election with anti-data-center candidates.
☐ Check proffer / development agreement / community benefit expectations
Why: In competitive jurisdictions, road improvements, fiscal contributions, and design proffers are the de facto price of approval. Budget them like hard costs.
Red flags: Recent comparable approvals carried proffers materially above your pro forma; jurisdiction has no development agreement statute (handshake risk).

Water, Sewer & Cooling (10%)

Cooling technology choices drive water demand, and water is the most politically combustible input after power — especially in the West.

☐ Quantify water demand under the actual cooling design (evaporative vs. closed-loop vs. air-cooled) and confirm supply
Why: Demand ranges from near-zero (air-cooled) to millions of gallons/day (evaporative). Get the will-serve from the water purveyor sized to the real design, with peak-day numbers.
Red flags: Purveyor capacity letter caps supply below peak design day; supply contingent on unbuilt treatment capacity.
☐ Confirm water rights regime and whether new appropriations are realistically available
Why: In prior-appropriation states, municipal supply may be the only practical path; new rights can be effectively unobtainable. Groundwater management areas add another layer.
Red flags: Site in a groundwater management/active management area with moratorium on new wells; water rights marketed with the land are unadjudicated or relinquishment-prone.
☐ Review sewer/industrial discharge capacity and pretreatment requirements for blowdown
Why: Cooling tower blowdown is an industrial discharge. POTW capacity and pretreatment permits are routinely overlooked until design development.
Red flags: POTW at or near permitted capacity; TDS limits that force on-site treatment.
☐ Assess reclaimed/recycled water availability as both cost saver and political mitigation
Why: Purple-pipe supply materially de-risks the public narrative and may be cheaper. Some jurisdictions now effectively require it for large users.
Red flags: Reclaimed main >3 miles away with no extension plan; reclaimed supply already fully subscribed.

Environmental (8%)

☐ Phase I ESA (and Phase II if RECs) under current ASTM standard, with non-scope items considered
Why: Baseline CERCLA innocent-landowner protection. For data centers, also ask the consultant to address PFAS (firefighting systems history) and historic agricultural chemical use.
Red flags: Former industrial/landfill/orchard use; dry cleaner or UST history on or adjacent; vapor pathways near planned office space.
☐ Wetlands/waters delineation and 404/401 permitting exposure
Why: Large-pad grading on greenfield sites frequently touches jurisdictional waters; mitigation banking costs and Corps timelines belong in the schedule and budget.
Red flags: Mapped streams/wetlands across the buildable area; recent jurisdictional rule changes in flux for the region.
☐ Threatened/endangered species and critical habitat screening
Why: A listed species survey window can freeze grading for a season. Know the survey calendar before signing a construction-start commitment.
Red flags: Site in mapped critical habitat; survey windows that don't align with the construction schedule.
☐ Air permitting pathway for backup generators (and any bridging generation)
Why: A 100+ MW campus can carry dozens of 2-3 MW diesel generators — minor source vs. major source determination drives a 6-24 month spread in timeline.
Red flags: Nonattainment designation; aggregation risk with neighboring facilities under common control.
☐ Stormwater, floodplain, and geotech screening
Why: Data halls are flat, heavy, and water-averse. FEMA floodplain on the pad or poor soils (expansive clay, shallow rock, high groundwater) move real money.
Red flags: Any floodway touch; karst terrain; groundwater within excavation depth of mission-critical vaults.

Title, Survey & Land (7%)

Standard CRE title work, but with data-center-specific traps: easement corridors, mineral severance under big pads, and assemblage seams.

☐ Full title commitment review with data-center lens on easements and use restrictions
Why: Pipeline, transmission, and fiber easements crossing the pad area are not minor exceptions here — they dictate building placement. CC&Rs with use or height restrictions can quietly prohibit the project.
Red flags: Blanket easements without defined location; private restrictions requiring third-party consent for industrial use; reversionary clauses tied to use.
☐ Mineral estate severance and surface-use risk
Why: In severed-estate states, the mineral owner's implied right of reasonable surface use is incompatible with a mission-critical facility. Surface waivers or accommodation agreements may be needed.
Red flags: Active leases or recent mineral conveyances; state with strong mineral-dominance doctrine; no surface waiver obtainable.
☐ ALTA survey with utility corridor, easement, and encroachment mapping against the conceptual site plan
Why: Overlay the survey on the power and fiber routing plan early — the cheapest time to discover a corridor conflict is before the deposit goes hard.
Red flags: Gaps or gores in an assemblage; boundary line agreements needed with multiple neighbors.
☐ Assemblage strategy: option structure, confidentiality, and holdout exposure
Why: Multi-parcel campuses live or die on sequencing. Options with staged exercise, name-shielded entities, and uniform pricing discipline prevent the last parcel from costing more than the first ten.
Red flags: Key center parcel not yet under control; broker leakage of end-user identity; recorded memoranda tipping the market.
☐ Existing agricultural/forestry tax classification and rollback/penalty exposure on conversion
Why: Current-use programs (ag deferral, forestland classification) trigger rollback taxes and penalties on change of use — a real number on 200+ acre sites, and allocation between buyer/seller belongs in the PSA.
Red flags: Decade-plus of deferral accumulated; statute imposes penalty in addition to rollback; PSA silent on allocation.

Tax & Incentives (10%)

☐ State sales/use tax exemption for data center equipment: eligibility thresholds, sunset dates, and clawbacks
Why: Equipment is 70-80% of project cost and refreshes every 4-6 years; the sales tax exemption is usually the single largest incentive. Eligibility thresholds (investment, jobs, wages) and certification timing are strict.
Red flags: Program sunset before refresh cycles; pending legislation to repeal or condition the exemption; jobs covenant the operating model can't meet.
☐ Property tax treatment of real vs. personal property, and abatement availability
Why: Classification of servers, generators, and electrical gear as real vs. personal property varies by state and drives the assessed base. Local abatements (PILOT, FILOT, Chapter-style agreements) are negotiable instruments — paper them like contracts.
Red flags: County reassessment cycle landing mid-construction; abatement requires annual reapplication; assessor history of aggressive 'fixtures' positions.
☐ Negotiated incentive agreements: performance covenants, clawbacks, transferability
Why: Incentive agreements are contracts with default provisions. Confirm they survive an asset sale or REIT transfer, and model the clawback as a contingent liability.
Red flags: Non-assignable incentives in a build-to-sell strategy; clawback triggered by headcount metrics outside operator control.
☐ Transfer tax / recording cost structuring on acquisition (entity vs. asset, applicable exemptions)
Why: On nine-figure land deals, real estate excise/transfer tax planning is real money. Controlling-interest transfer rules catch entity deals in several states.
Red flags: State with controlling-interest transfer tax and lookback period; county-level surcharge recently enacted.
☐ Utility tax, gross receipts tax, and franchise fee exposure on power purchases
Why: A facility buying $100M+/yr of electricity cares about every percentage point of utility tax. Some municipalities tax utility sales at 5-6%; site selection inside vs. outside city limits can be an eight-figure NPV decision.
Red flags: Annexation risk into a high-utility-tax city; gross receipts tax regimes reaching colocation revenue.

Fiber & Connectivity (5%)

☐ Identify long-haul and metro fiber routes serving the site and distance to splice points
Why: Two diverse fiber paths is the standard. Lit buildings nearby help, but what matters is conduit rights and construction cost to reach diverse backbone routes.
Red flags: Single fiber corridor serving the area; railroad or limited-access highway crossing required for diversity.
☐ Confirm rights-of-way and franchise/permit pathway for new fiber laterals
Why: Fiber laterals cross public ROW and private land; county franchise requirements and railroad crossing agreements have their own timelines (railroads are famously slow).
Red flags: Railroad crossing on every diverse-path option; private easements needed from uncooperative neighbors.
☐ Latency profile to relevant exchange points / availability zones
Why: For some workloads proximity to existing cloud regions or IXPs is a leasing prerequisite; for AI training it matters less. Know the target tenant before pricing this in.
Red flags: Marketing the site for latency-sensitive use at distances the physics doesn't support.

Community, Political & Legal Risk (5%)

Data centers have become a local political issue in a way warehouses never were. Quiet sites get loud fast.

☐ Map the opposition landscape: organized groups, recent contested projects, local press tone
Why: A jurisdiction's last data center fight predicts your hearing. Search local coverage, planning commission minutes, and citizen group activity before relying on a 'business friendly' label.
Red flags: Active anti-data-center citizen group; prior project withdrawn or denied; moratorium adopted in an adjacent jurisdiction (contagion).
☐ Review pending state legislation touching data centers (tariffs, incentives, siting, water, noise)
Why: Legislative sessions have produced large-load tariff mandates, incentive repeals, and siting bills with short fuses. A pending bill can change the deal between LOI and closing.
Red flags: Bill with momentum that conditions incentives on ratepayer protections your pro forma didn't assume.
☐ Check for litigation risk patterns: land use appeals, NEPA/state-EIS challenges, ratepayer complaints
Why: Serial land-use appellants and well-funded environmental petitioners can add 12-24 months even when they lose. Price the delay, not just the merits.
Red flags: State with liberal land-use standing rules and a history of data center appeals; utility's certificate proceedings already contested.
☐ Election calendar and key-decision-maker mapping
Why: Approvals that span an election are hostage to it. Know which seats turn over before your hearing dates and what the candidates are saying about data centers.
Red flags: Project approval scheduled within 90 days of a contested local election where the project is an issue.
