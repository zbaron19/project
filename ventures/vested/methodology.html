Data sourcing

Records are compiled from, in order of preference:

  • Official government sources: county and municipal ordinance/resolution texts, agenda packets, planning department project pages, and adopted-text repositories (e.g., Loudoun County's Data Center Standards & Locations page, Prince William County's planning office DPA pages, DeKalb County agenda PDFs).
  • Law firm client alerts from land use practices actively appearing in these matters (Holland & Knight, McGuireWoods, Venable, Bean Kinney & Korman, and others). These are secondary sources but tend to be precise on instrument numbers, effective dates, and transition rules.
  • Local press of record (e.g., Texas Tribune, AJC, GPB, Fauquier Now, Prince William Times, regional broadcast outlets) for votes, tallies, hearing dates, and litigation developments.

Every record carries at least one source URL, placed adjacent to the substantive fields it supports. Where we relied on a secondary source for a legally significant detail (an ordinance number, a carve-out's exact reach), the record says so.

Verification method

Each record's verification_method field currently reads "manual review of cited public sources." That means a human read the cited materials and transcribed the facts; it does not mean we have independently obtained certified copies of every ordinance. Fields we could not confirm are stored as null and rendered as "Not yet verified" — we never fill gaps with plausible-looking ordinance numbers, dates, or vote tallies. Where a status label is an imperfect fit (e.g., a rezoning judicially voided ab initio shown under the “repealed” filter), the scope decode states the precise posture.

Update cadence

Weekly. Covered jurisdictions are re-checked weekly against agendas, dockets, and local press; each record's last_verified date is refreshed when re-checked, and changed_date is updated only when the underlying government action actually changed. The “Changed this week” view is derived strictly from changed_date — if nothing changed, the view is empty. We do not manufacture news to fill a newsletter.

Per-state vesting primer

The single most consequential question in any moratorium or downzoning fight is whether a project's rights had already vested when the rules changed. The doctrine differs sharply by state. This primer is orientation, not advice; the cases turn on facts and local procedure.

Texas — statutory vesting at first filing

Tex. Loc. Gov't Code ch. 245 freezes the regulatory regime for a project at the filing of the first permit application in a series. File a preliminary plat (or other qualifying first application) and, in general, later-adopted ordinances cannot be applied to that project. Two practical corollaries dominate Texas records in this tracker: (1) a Texas moratorium mostly governs projects that have not yet started their permit chain, so the race is to file anything qualifying before adoption; and (2) Texas counties — unlike home-rule cities — have no general zoning power and only the authority statutes expressly give them, which is why Hill County's 2026 county-level moratorium drew an immediate authority-based lawsuit and was rescinded within three weeks. Cities adopting moratoria must also follow the procedural requirements of Loc. Gov't Code ch. 212, subch. E (hearings and findings), as Harlingen did.

Virginia — statutory vesting, but only after a "significant affirmative governmental act"

Va. Code § 15.2-2307 vests rights only where there is (1) a significant affirmative governmental act — an approved rezoning with proffers, an approved special exception or special use permit, an approved site plan, and the like — plus (2) good-faith reliance and (3) substantial expense in furtherance of the project. A pending application generally does not vest in Virginia. That is why Virginia jurisdictions tightening their ordinances (Loudoun's ZOAM-2024-0001, Fairfax's 2024 amendment, Prince William's DCOZOD reduction) negotiate explicit grandfathering resolutions — the statute will not save in-process projects on its own. Note Virginia terminology: the discretionary instrument is a special exception or special use permit (some localities label theirs a conditional use permit, but the state-law concept is the SUP/special exception); "CUP" usage from other states does not map cleanly. A separate Virginia lesson from the Digital Gateway litigation: an approval that is void ab initio (e.g., for defective public notice under Va. Code § 15.2-2204) is treated as never having existed and cannot anchor vesting at all.

Georgia — common-law vesting at permit or substantial expenditure

Georgia has no chapter-245-style statute. Vested rights arise at common law: generally upon issuance of a building permit, or upon substantial expenditures made in good-faith reliance on existing zoning (Georgia courts have in some circumstances recognized reliance on the probability of permit issuance). The practical consequence shows up across this tracker's Georgia records: a moratorium that pauses acceptance of applications (Newton) is far less dangerous to in-flight projects than one that blocks permit issuance and expansions (DeKalb) — under Georgia doctrine, the zoned-but-unpermitted project is the exposed one. Georgia uses county and municipal zoning side by side, and annexation into a city can change the applicable regime mid-project, an arbitrage developers actively use in metro Atlanta.

Other states appearing in the tracker

Where a record sits outside the three primer states (e.g., Kentucky's Oldham County), the record's vesting note is orientation only and is flagged as such. Kentucky, like Georgia, follows common-law principles that generally require an issued permit plus substantial reliance — which is how Oldham County could deliberately sweep an already-filed application into its moratorium by striking the draft carve-out.

Why the carve-out field gets its own callout

Most moratoria expressly exempt complete applications already on file when the pause takes effect. Whether yours does — and whether "on file" means accepted, complete, or merely submitted — is frequently the entire dispute. Vested quotes the operative carve-out language where we have it, paraphrases (and says so) where we do not, and flags the counter-examples: Oldham County, KY struck its pending-application exemption on the record; DeKalb County, GA wrote its pause to reach permit issuance and expansions, not just new filings.

Status vocabulary

  • Action types: moratorium, ordinance amendment, overlay district, comprehensive plan, staff pause, litigation.
  • Instrument kinds: ordinance, resolution, staff action — these are legally distinct (an ordinance amends law; a resolution often expresses policy or imposes a temporary pause; a staff action may have no legislative footing at all), and we label them honestly rather than calling everything an "ordinance."
  • Statuses: proposed, adopted, extended, expired, repealed, pending litigation. Judicial invalidation is shown under "repealed" for filtering, with the precise posture stated in the record.

Corrections

If you find an error — a wrong date, a misread carve-out, a stale status — we want it. Corrections ship in the next weekly cycle and the record's change log notes the fix.
